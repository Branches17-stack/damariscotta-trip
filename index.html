Damariscotta Lake

4,381 acres, 10 miles long, 21 miles of shoreline. Mean depth 30 ft, max 114 ft. Three basins, two fisheries. Chart drawn from the Maine IF&W survey.

IF&W depth map
Tap a basin on the chart or a button for what lives there and how to fish it Friday.

How the lake is built

BasinAreaMax depthAugust survey tempsCharacter
North2,129 acres114 ft73°F surface, 45°F at 110 ftDeep, rocky, coldwater. The house, the state park, Davis Stream.
Middle1,463 acres80 ft73°F surface, 53°F at 59 ftIslands, points, the Muscongus Bay arm. Bass water.
South789 acres38 ft76°F surface, 48°F at 35 ftNarrow, weedy arm down to the outlet at Damariscotta Mills.

The middle basin is deeper than it looks from shore. Eighty feet of water with 53°F at sixty means salmon and togue use it too, not just the north basin. Bass hold on the edges where that deep water comes up against the islands.

Two things from the survey worth knowing. Largemouth were never stocked; they got in through an unauthorized introduction in the early 1980s and settled in the shallow south basin. And the lake is the breeding ground for one of the biggest alewife runs in Maine, over 100,000 fish in 1995, which is why the bass and salmon here eat so well. IF&W's own line is that the bass fishery probably contributes more to the fishing than every other species combined.

Forage: rainbow smelt, golden shiners, fallfish, sea-run alewives. Also present: white sucker, banded killifish, pumpkinseed, American eel.

What late September does to the fish

Surface temperature is 69 to 71°F and dropping a degree or two a week. The August survey found 53°F at 59 feet in the middle basin and 45°F at 110 feet in the north basin, so the coldwater fish spend summer well below reach on light gear. As the surface cools the thermocline breaks down and salmon, browns, and togue push up into the 20 to 40 foot band, especially at first light and in wind. It's early for the full fall turnover but the trend is in your favor.

Smallmouth are in their fall feeding push. Smelt and young alewives are schooled up along points and drop-offs, and the bass sit on the first break below them, usually 8 to 20 feet. They're aggressive and they group up, so when you find one there are more.

Largemouth are still tight to green weed in the south basin. Weed dies off from the shallows out, so the outside edge of the healthiest weed is the line to work.

Friday game plan

Rain and low pressure in the morning is the best bite window of the day for bass and the only realistic window for a salmon on light gear. Start close to the house in the north basin: work the rocky points and the first drop-off either side of the dock with jigs, and put one trolling rod out with a streamer or small spoon if the canoe is going anywhere.

Once the rain lifts and the light comes up, run south to the islands. Fish the windward side of each island and the points that stick out into the narrows. Drag a tube or Ned rig along the bottom, then switch to a drop shot when you find the depth they're holding.

After lunch, if the bass bite slows in the flat afternoon light, the south basin weed edges are the numbers play: pickerel and perch will hit almost anything moving, and a largemouth off a dock is worth the same as a smallmouth.

Wind: a south or southwest breeze pushes bait onto the north-facing shores. Fish the bank the wind is blowing into. If it's flat calm, go deeper and slower.

Tackle by target

  • Smallmouth: 1/8 to 3/8 oz lead-head jigs with yellow sparkle-tail or curly-tail grubs (the local standard), tubes, Ned rigs, drop shot with a 3 to 4 inch finesse worm, a small crankbait for covering water.
  • Largemouth: Texas-rigged worms and creatures on the weed edge, a spinnerbait along the outside of the weed line, a frog or popper over the pads at first light.
  • Pickerel and perch: inline spinners, Mickey Finn streamers, small jigs tipped with a piece of worm.
  • Salmon, browns, togue: troll a streamer (Grey Ghost, Black Ghost) or a smelt-pattern spoon on long line at first light; go deeper and slower off the state park for togue.

Scoring

  • Each fish: 1 point plus 1 point per inch, to the nearest quarter inch. Minimum 8 inches to count.
  • Multiplier by species: smallmouth and largemouth x1; perch and pickerel x½; salmon, brown trout, togue x2.
  • First fish of the day +5. Three or more species +10.
  • Ties: biggest bass, then first fish.

Why the half-multiplier: a 10-inch perch at full value is 11 points, and a school of them off the dock would out-score one 18-inch smallmouth (19 points) in ten minutes. At half value it takes four decent perch to match one good bass, which feels right. A 16-inch salmon is still 34 points, so the deep-water gamble pays if it lands.
